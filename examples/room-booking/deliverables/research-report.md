# Research report — Online study-room booking for the town library

**Prepared by:** Business Analyst · **Date:** 10 October 2026 · **Status:** revised draft for QC review (round 5; the changes are listed in the requirements specification's change history, versions 0.2 to 0.5)
**Inputs:** `brief.md`; `elicitation/answers-round-1.md`; `elicitation/elicitation-record.md` (brief facts B-1 to B-6, adopted defaults Q-1 to Q-15 and AS-01 to AS-23).
**How to read this report:** `[S#]` points to the source register in §11. `F-n` is a numbered finding in §5, `R-n` a risk in §8 and `C-n` a conclusion in §10. Must, Should and Could are the MoSCoW priorities used in the requirements specification (essential, important, desirable). Figures marked **estimate** are worked out by the analyst, with the basis stated beside each. F-34 and C-18 were added in revision; F-34 sits in §5.5, the section it answers. Terms for the member sign-in limit follow the specification's glossary: a **sign-in pause** is stage 1 (online sign-in for one card refused for at least 15 minutes after every 5th consecutive failure), and the **overall cap** is stage 2 (online sign-in for the card stops until staff re-enable it). A **block** is only ever a period in which staff make a room unavailable, and an **LMS block** is a status on a member's account in the library management system (LMS).

## 1. Executive summary

**The question.** The library's six study rooms are booked by phone or at the front desk; at exam time the desk is overwhelmed, and around a quarter of booked rooms sit empty because people don't turn up [S1]. The owner wants members to see free rooms and book them themselves, asked how other libraries do this and what members expect [S1], and approved all 15 round-1 defaults, including a no-show target of 12% or less in May 2027 [S2].

**What the evidence says.**
- Public libraries that offer online booking keep desk, staff or phone routes alongside it. Libraries typically release a room if the booker hasn't arrived or checked in within 15–30 minutes, and suspend members who repeatedly don't turn up (F-1 to F-3).
- Reminders raise attendance by about 11–14% in healthcare trials, so on their own they would probably cut no-shows from about 25% to roughly 14–17% (**estimate**); automatic release recovers room time without lowering the rate, so reaching 12% also needs easy cancellation and the deterrent of suspension, which no study has evaluated (F-7 to F-11).
- Booking products made for libraries already offer sign-in with the library card number and personal identification number (PIN), booking rules, reminders, check-in and reports (F-12, F-16). General booking tools, including the UK products listed on the government's G-Cloud 15 buying framework, don't offer library sign-in (F-14, F-27).
- The usual link for checking library sign-ins, SIP2 (Standard Interchange Protocol version 2), sends members' details, including PINs, in plain text unless encrypted (F-17). A public card-and-PIN sign-in page must cap wrong guesses in total, not just slow them, because the same PIN opens members' other library services (F-34).
- Ofcom found that 6% of UK households had no home internet in 2023, and 7–8% of adults lack essential digital skills, with older people over-represented (F-6). Online booking can't be the only route.
- Public-sector accessibility rules require the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2 at level AA and an accessibility statement, including for staff-only screens bought now, even when a supplier runs the service (F-19).

**Recommendation.** Buy and configure a hosted booking service made for libraries rather than build one, and keep desk and phone booking in the same calendar. Against no-shows, use three measures together: reminders that state the consequence of not coming, with one-click cancellation; check-in with automatic release; and suspension after repeated no-shows, with notice and staff review. Trade-off: a yearly subscription and reliance on the library-system supplier for an encrypted sign-in link, in exchange for a faster, lower-risk launch before the May 2027 peak (C-1 to C-4).

**Key implications for the requirements.** Three change the round-1 defaults: WCAG 2.2 AA for staff screens (C-7) and notice of no-shows and suspensions with a route to staff review (C-8) rise from Should to Must, and member sign-in gets a two-stage limit on wrong PINs: a sign-in pause of at least 15 minutes after every 5 failures, then no online sign-in after 20 until staff re-enable it (C-18). The rest confirm the defaults or add security detail (§10).

**Key risks.** The 12% target may be missed, because the evidence comes from healthcare (C-3). Members who forget to check in will be recorded as no-shows (C-5). There are under five months to buy, set up and test (C-13). A printed QR (quick response) code can be photographed and used elsewhere (C-14). Even with a cap of 20 guesses, a stranger could find roughly 5–8% of PINs that members chose themselves (**estimate**, F-34), and the cap lets someone shut members out until staff step in (C-18).

**Decisions needed.** Owner: keep the 15-minute release or relax it to 20–30 minutes (C-4); keep or revise the 12% target (C-3). Data protection officer: judge whether the automatic suspension is a significant automated decision (C-8). Library systems staff: name the library management system and confirm an encrypted interface (C-6); say whether it locks PINs after failed attempts, how PINs are set and how long they are (C-18). Council IT: validate the cap of 20 and accept the risk that remains, because no cap brings a short PIN used alone up to the US digital identity standard, NIST SP 800-63B-4 (C-18). Procurement: confirm the buying route (C-13).

**Confidence.** High on legal and accessibility duties; medium on product capabilities (supplier claims); low to medium on how far no-shows will fall (healthcare evidence).

## 2. Context and objectives

**The problem as stated.** "Members book the library's six study rooms by phoning or visiting the front desk. At exam time the desk is overwhelmed, and around a quarter of booked rooms sit empty because people don't turn up." [S1]

**What the owner wants.** "A way for members to see which rooms are free and book them themselves, with fewer empty rooms." [S1]

**Facts given.** About 4,000 active members; roughly 300 room bookings a month, peaking in May and December; a public library in England that must meet public-sector accessibility rules; an existing library management system holds member accounts [S1].

**The owner's answers.** The owner did not answer the round-1 questions and approved every stated default [S2]. The defaults that shape this research are: no-shows at or below 12% in May 2027, with at least 60% of bookings made by members (Q-1); reminders, check-in with release after 15 minutes, and a 4-week suspension after 3 no-shows in 90 days, with no fees (Q-2); free rooms for active members with no LMS block (Q-3); 1-hour slots, at most 3 hours, up to 14 days ahead and at most 2 future bookings, with a stricter peak rule set (Q-4); desk, phone and walk-in booking kept (Q-5); an off-the-shelf hosted product live by 1 March 2027 (Q-6); a quotation exercise naming no supplier (Q-7); card number and PIN sign-in checked against the library management system (Q-8); check-in by printed QR code (Q-9); UK or adequacy-covered hosting, Cyber Essentials, multi-factor staff sign-in and a data protection impact assessment (DPIA) (Q-10); and email confirmation and reminder, with text messages as a Should (Q-11). **What this research had to establish.**

2. Whether the evidence supports or contradicts the bases given for the round-1 defaults (`elicitation-record.md` §2).

4. The legal, accessibility and data-protection duties of a public library in England, including whether staff screens fall under the accessibility regulations (B-4).
5. Whether the working service levels in AS-14 (availability) and AS-15 (speed) are reasonable, and how serious the photographed-QR-code risk flagged under Q-9 is.
6. Which security baseline a hosted service holding members' data should meet, and how to handle sensitive details staff might record (Q-10, AS-06). Added while writing the requirements specification.

## 4. Method

**Where I searched.** Web searches on 10 October 2026, followed by fetching each source. Source types, in order of preference:
- Regulators and government: legislation.gov.uk; GOV.UK pages of the Department for Culture, Media and Sport (DCMS), the Department for Science, Innovation and Technology (DSIT), the Cabinet Office and the Government Digital Service (GDS); the Information Commissioner's Office (ICO); the National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC); and the Government Commercial Agency (GCA), including searches of the G-Cloud 15 catalogue on the government's Digital Marketplace.
- Standards bodies: the World Wide Web Consortium (W3C), the National Information Standards Organization (NISO) and the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST).
- Industry security guidance: the cheat sheets of the Open Worldwide Application Security Project (OWASP), a non-profit security community.
- Peer-reviewed research: a Cochrane systematic review, a 2026 meta-analysis, randomised trials, library journals and two security studies of PINs that people choose.
- Library policy pages of public and university libraries in England, Canada and the United States.
- Supplier documents, used only for what a product claims to do, and labelled as such.
- Reputable press and practitioner material, used only where no primary source was available, and labelled.

**Inclusion criteria.** Credible publisher; directly relevant; published from October 2023 onwards where possible. Older sources are kept only when they are legislation, a standard, a foundational study or the only evidence available, and are labelled "older" or "foundational" in §11.

**Verification.** I fetched every source before citing it and cited only what the fetched text says. Sources I could not open were left out, even where search snippets described them: Sutton Council's study-space page (access refused), Ofcom's 2026 media-use report (access refused), the Technical University of Munich's booking-survey pages (not found), an Illinois usability study (access refused) and DataGenetics' 2012 analysis of leaked PINs (expired security certificate).

**Date range.** Legislation from 1964 to 2025; core evidence published 2023–2026; older studies (2012–2023) used where no newer evidence exists, and flagged.

**F-1 Public libraries keep several booking routes.** Each public library above that offers online booking also takes bookings or cancellations by phone, in person or through staff [S3][S4][S6], or handles check-in at the desk [S5]. This supports the Q-5 default of one calendar shared by online, desk and phone booking.

**F-2 Unclaimed rooms are released 15–30 minutes after the start.** Observed windows: 15 minutes [S4], 20 minutes [S7] and 30 minutes [S8][S3]. The Q-2 default of 15 minutes sits at the strict end of this range.

**F-3 Suspension after repeated no-shows is common, and its length varies.** One month after repeated no-shows [S3]; up to three months after three in a calendar year, with a written appeal [S4]; and a suspension after four missed bookings in a week, where advance cancellations and bookings that couldn't be cancelled (for example through illness) don't count, and existing bookings are kept [S10][S11]. The Q-2 default (three no-shows in 90 days, four weeks) falls inside this range, and its staff-correction and kept-bookings rules (Q-2, AS-05) match the Bodleian's exemptions.

**F-4 Booking limits cluster around two weeks ahead and two to four hours a day.** Advance windows run from 5 days [S3] to 1 month [S4], with two English universities at 14 days or 2 weeks [S7][S9]. Length caps run from 2 hours a day [S5], or 2 hours a session, extendable on request [S4], to 3 hours a day [S8] and 4 hours a day [S3]; one library also caps bookings per week [S9]. The Q-4 defaults (14 days ahead, at most 3 hours, at most 2 future bookings) fall within these ranges.

**Gap.** I found and verified only one English public library that takes study-space bookings online, and it charges for small pods [S6]. English public-library practice is therefore less documented than university and North American public-library practice (§9).

### 5.2 RQ-2: What members expect, and who risks being left out

**F-5 Members expect clear room information, a clear confirmation and an easy way to cancel.** Usability testing found students wanted images and descriptions of each space, opening hours, and a filter that hides spaces they can't book [S12]. An older test found users needed a clear confirmation that the booking had been made and a clear sense of where they were in the time grid [S13]. Libraries routinely send a confirmation email with a cancellation link [S8][S5][S3][S9]. This supports Q-11 (confirmation with a cancellation link) and Q-15 (room details on each booking page).

**F-6 A sizeable minority can't or won't book online.** The government's Digital Inclusion Action Plan reports Lloyds' estimate that 1.6 million people in the UK are living offline and Ofcom's finding that 6% of UK households had no internet access at home in 2023, and states that 3.8 million people (7%) lack the essential digital skills for life [S14]. A 2025 survey for Lloyds Banking Group found 92% of UK adults have essential digital skills for life, so about 8% do not, with older people over-represented among those with lower ability [S15]. In a YouGov poll for the University of Strathclyde (fieldwork November 2021), 57% of adults disagreed that digital services are an adequate replacement for traditional library services, rising to 64% among people aged 55 and over [S16]. Government service guidance expects assisted-digital support and research with people who have access needs [S17]. This supports keeping desk and phone booking and staff check-in (Q-5, Q-9) and testing with disabled and older members (Q-14).

### 5.3 RQ-3: What reduces no-shows and empty rooms

**F-7 Reminders raise attendance modestly.** A Cochrane review of 8 randomised trials (6,615 people) compared text reminders with no reminder in 7 of them (5,841 people): attendance rose from 67.8% to 78.6% (risk ratio 1.14, 95% confidence interval 1.03–1.26; moderate-quality evidence). In 3 trials (2,509 people), text reminders worked as well as phone reminders (moderate-quality evidence) [S18]. A risk ratio of 1.14 means people who got a reminder were 14% more likely to attend than people who didn't. The 95% confidence interval is the range the true effect plausibly lies in; when it includes 1 (no difference), the result is not statistically significant. A 2026 meta-analysis of 10 studies (8,236 people) found reminders of all kinds raised attendance (risk ratio 1.11, 95% confidence interval 1.05–1.19). For text reminders alone the result was not statistically significant (risk ratio 1.14, 0.99–1.31; p = 0.07, where p is the chance of a difference at least this large if reminders had no effect, and by convention must be below 0.05), and results varied widely between studies [S19]. Neither review covers email reminders or settings outside healthcare.

**F-8 Reminder wording matters.** In two National Health Service (NHS) outpatient trials in London, a text that stated the specific cost of a missed appointment cut non-attendance from 11.1% to 8.4% (odds ratio 0.74: the odds of missing an appointment were about a quarter lower). A version that described the cost only in general terms did less well (9.9% against 8.2%) [S20]. This is one study at one hospital trust, so it is a pointer rather than a key finding: reminders should state the concrete consequence ("if you can't come, cancel so another member can use the room") and carry a one-click cancellation link.

**F-9 Reminders alone are unlikely to reach the 12% target (estimate).** Basis: about 25% no-shows today means about 75% of bookings are used [S1]. Applying the pooled risk ratios of 1.11–1.14 from F-7 gives about 83–86% attendance, so roughly 14–17% no-shows. This assumes healthcare effects carry over to free library rooms, which is unproven. Check-in and release don't close the gap: release recovers room time (F-10), but a released booking still counts as a no-show. Check-in is what makes no-shows countable, so that suspension can be enforced. Only reminders, easy cancellation (a cancelled booking is not a no-show) and the deterrent of suspension, which is unevaluated (F-11), can lower the no-show rate towards the Q-1 target of 12%.

**F-10 Check-in and automatic release recover room time but don't stop people failing to turn up.** Release returns an unclaimed room to others within 15–30 minutes (F-2). Libraries expect some members to forget to check in: Newcastle warns that "if you forget to scan in, the system will automatically cancel your booking after 20 minutes" [S7], and Sussex cancels the booking if the member doesn't scan within 30 minutes [S8]. I found no controlled study of how much check-in raises room use (§9).

**F-11 Evidence on suspensions is practice, not evaluation.** The Bodleian introduced its suspension rule after 61% of readers (1,214) missed at least one reading-room booking without cancelling in a single week in 2021, during pandemic seat booking [S10][S11]. Both reports rest on the same Bodleian statement, and no source evaluates whether suspensions change behaviour. Libraries that suspend also build in fairness: exemptions for advance cancellations and for bookings that couldn't be cancelled, for example through illness [S10], or a written appeal [S4].

**Fees and deposits.** Some libraries charge for bookable space [S6], and at least one library booking product supports charging [S21]. Fees are out of scope under the owner's defaults (Q-2(d), Q-3), so I did not research their effect.

### 5.4 RQ-4: What solutions exist

**F-12 Booking products made for libraries already cover the core needs.** Two independent suppliers document the functions the brief and defaults require:
- Springshare LibCal: members "enter their library card number and PIN" to book spaces; booking mediation and room settings; text-message reminders as an add-on; tablets outside rooms for on-the-spot booking and check-in; smart-lock integration; and reports on space use [S22]. It also offers check-in and check-out, automatic reminder emails, configurable booking rules and limits, and a mobile-friendly interface [S23]. Staff can look up a member's details in the library system over SIP2, or directly in the Alma and Sierra library systems [S24], and booking can be limited to chosen user groups [S25]. Springshare runs separate data-centre clusters in the United States, the European Union, Australia and Canada [S26].
- Communico Reserve: integration with the library system "for patron authentication, age verification and card blocks"; booking limits; confirmations and reminders by email, text or push notification; tablets outside rooms; door locks that issue codes valid only during the booking; custom reports; and optional charging [S21]. Staff can book on a member's behalf, and a library card can be required for all bookings [S27].

These are supplier claims, not tested capabilities. Neither of these two suppliers publishes prices on the pages reviewed.

**F-13 The library-system supplier may not sell a room-booking module.** For Civica Spydus, a library management system Civica says is trusted by more than 1,500 libraries, the product page lists event management and an interface that checks member sign-in, but no room or space booking [S28]. Because the library's own system isn't named (Q-8), I could not check its supplier (§9).

**F-14 General booking tools, including UK products sold to libraries, lack library sign-in.** Skedda offers a check-in option and single sign-on for organisations, priced from $99 a month billed annually for 15 spaces in Europe, the Middle East and Africa (the page doesn't name the currency). It doesn't list library-system sign-in, and the page says nothing about accessibility [S29]. Microsoft Bookings is built for appointments with staff, sends email and text notifications, and comes with many Microsoft 365 and Office 365 plans, including government plans [S30]. A university pilot found a free calendar tool workable but "very basic", needing "considerably more upkeep and management by staff" than paid or open-source options [S31]. Two UK booking products sold to councils and libraries are listed on G-Cloud 15. LemonBooking publishes prices ("Plans start at £360 a year for Silver, billed annually"), and says booking data is stored in a UK data centre and that it is Cyber Essentials certified [S78]. Its G-Cloud 15 listing confirms the UK data location and the Cyber Essentials certificate, lists sign-in by username or password with multi-factor authentication, and states "The backend (staff-only) is not WCAG compliant" [S79]. SIM Resource Booking, listed as "Ideal for NHS, councils, schools, libraries", offers check-in and check-out, UK hosting and WCAG 2.2 AA, with username and password or single sign-on; its listing gives no price for the service [S80]. Neither the LemonBooking page nor either listing mentions library card and PIN sign-in, SIP2 or automatic release of unclaimed bookings [S78][S79][S80].

**F-15 Claims that online booking saves staff time come from suppliers.** Microsoft says Bookings reduces "repetitive scheduling tasks" [S30], and Communico says Reserve saves staff time by using one application for all room availability [S21]. I found no independent measure of front-desk time saved (§9).

§6 compares the options, including doing nothing.

### 5.5 RQ-5: Checking membership against the library management system

**F-16 Card number and PIN is the normal sign-in for English library accounts, and library booking products support it.** Hackney [S32], Wokingham [S33] and Liverpool [S34] councils all give members a card number and PIN for online library services. LibCal asks for card number and PIN when booking [S22], and Communico Reserve checks members against the library system, including "card blocks" (LMS blocks) [S21]. This supports the Q-8 default.

**F-17 SIP2, the usual link, is unencrypted unless protected.** 3M introduced the Standard Interchange Protocol (SIP) in 1993; it became the de facto standard for linking library management systems with self-service systems, and in 2012 3M passed it to NISO for formal standardisation [S35]. A library consortium's security briefing calls SIP2 "the industry standard for library authentication", notes that it has no built-in encryption and sends all data as plain text, and shows that a sign-in reply can carry the member's name, home address, email, date of birth and PIN. The consortium no longer sets up external SIP2 connections unless they support encryption with TLS (Transport Layer Security), the standard internet encryption protocol [S36]. A senior system administrator at an open-source library-system supplier, quoted on a practitioners' mailing list, gives the same advice: "there isn't really anything that can be done to SIP2 to make it secure without making it not-SIP2", so it must run inside an encrypted tunnel agreed with the supplier [S37]. Both sources are older (2021–2022), but they describe the protocol itself, which has not changed.

**F-18 Supplier interfaces are an alternative, and every connection needs work on the library-system side.** Spydus offers an application programming interface (API) that supports member sign-in checks [S28]. The consortium briefing notes that some products connect through a library-system supplier's own web services instead of SIP2, and that each new supplier connection needs its own set-up on the library-system server [S36]. This confirms the Q-8 dependency on the library-system supplier.

**F-34 A public card-and-PIN sign-in page must cap wrong guesses in total, not just slow them.** The booking service checks the card number and PIN live against the library management system from a page anyone can reach (Q-8). The same PIN opens members' other online library services: Hackney says "You'll need your library card number and PIN" [S32], and Wokingham asks for "your Personal Identification (PIN) to access services" [S33].

What the standards say:
- **NIST SP 800-63B-4**, part of the US federal Digital Identity Guidelines, says the verifier "SHALL limit consecutive failed authentication attempts … on a single subscriber account to no more than 100 by disabling that authenticator". A disabled authenticator must be re-bound to the account before it can be used again, and organisations may set a lower limit. After a successful sign-in, earlier failures should be disregarded and the count reset. Waits that lengthen as the limit nears ("30 seconds up to an hour") and bot-detection challenges may be used to make it less likely that an attacker locks the real user out [S76]. The 100 is therefore a total at which sign-in stops, not a rate. The same standard calls a numeric password a PIN and requires a password used as the only factor to be at least 15 characters (§3.1.1, §3.1.1.2) [S76]. A library PIN therefore falls short of NIST as a single factor whatever the cap; the limit reduces the risk but does not bring the sign-in up to NIST.
- **The NCSC** prefers throttling, "a progressively increasing time delay between successive login attempts". Where accounts lock, it recommends allowing "between 5 and 10 login attempts before the account is frozen". It warns that lockout "can leave legitimate users unable to access their accounts" and "can provide an attacker with an easy way to launch a denial of service attack" [S85].
- **OWASP's Authentication Cheat Sheet** advises that "The counter of failed logins should be associated with the account itself, rather than the source IP address". After at most 2–3 timed lockouts, it says the lockout should become permanent [S86].

Two consequences follow for the design of the limit:
- **A sign-in pause that simply repeats is not enough.** It caps how fast someone can guess, not how many guesses they get. A limit therefore needs two stages: short sign-in pauses (the NCSC's throttling and OWASP's timed lockouts), and then an overall cap at which online sign-in for the card stops until staff re-enable it, as NIST requires. The staff route matters because the cap is what lets a stranger shut a member out.
- **A per-card limit doesn't stop one PIN being tried on many cards.** OWASP's cheat sheets call this "password spraying": "Testing a single weak password against a large number of different accounts". An overall cap also lets someone shut many members out at once. OWASP's Credential Stuffing Prevention Cheat Sheet answers with layers: "multiple, layered defenses can provide a reasonable degree of protection". It suggests asking for a CAPTCHA (a test that tells people from automated programs) only "when the login request is considered suspicious or high risk". Refusing all requests from a network address "should not be used as the sole or primary defense" and "should be temporary" [S87].

**How much a cap protects depends on how PINs are chosen, more than on their length.** People don't choose PINs at random:
- In a peer-reviewed study, 851 people recruited online chose 4-digit PINs for unlocking a phone. An attacker allowed 10 guesses found 5% of them, and one allowed 30 found 8%. Six-digit PINs were guessed no less often (7% and 9%) [S88].
- A peer-reviewed study of banking PINs estimated that a thief who guesses from the birth date on documents in a stolen wallet would get to use a card once for every 11–18 wallets, depending on whether the bank prohibits weak PINs such as 1234 [S89].
- Some libraries set the PIN by default. UCL Library says "Your PIN is four digits and for UCL students is set automatically to your date of birth (Day/Day/Month/Month)" [S90]. Such a PIN has at most 366 values, and anyone who knows the member's birthday knows it.

So a cap of 20 would let a stranger find roughly 5–8% of 4-digit PINs that members chose themselves (**estimate**: between the study's 10- and 30-guess figures [S88]). The 0.2% that 20 of 10,000 combinations suggests applies only if PINs were random. The study's PINs were chosen for phones, not library accounts, so the figure is indicative. A date-of-birth default would protect far less [S89][S90]. NIST's limit on failed attempts allows any cap of 100 or less (§3.2.2) [S76], and how PINs are set is the main thing to weigh when choosing it.

Any bot check must still meet WCAG 2.2 success criterion 3.3.8 (§7) [S63]. It isn't known whether the library's own system locks a PIN after failed checks, how its PINs are set (chosen by members, a default such as the date of birth, or with common PINs refused) or how long they are (§9).

### 5.6 RQ-6: Legal, regulatory, standards and accessibility duties

The full list, with titles and versions, is in §7. The findings that change or confirm requirements are:

**F-19 Accessibility duties cover staff screens too, and outsourcing doesn't move responsibility.** Public-sector websites and apps must meet WCAG 2.2 AA and publish an accessibility statement; "intranet and extranet websites are covered by the accessibility regulations"; and "you're legally responsible for your website meeting accessibility requirements" when a supplier runs it [S38]. The regulations define intranets and extranets as sites "only available for a closed group of people", and exempt only those published before 23 September 2019, until substantially revised. Third-party content is exempt only if it is neither funded nor developed by, nor under the control of, the public body [S39]. A staff booking screen bought now is new and paid for, so it is covered. This changes the planned priority for staff screens from Should to Must (C-7).

**F-20 Data protection law changed in 2025–2026, and the booking design meets several ICO triggers.** The Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 (DUAA) received Royal Assent on 19 June 2025 [S40]; its main data-protection changes came into force on 5 February 2026 [S41]. Under the amended UK General Data Protection Regulation (UK GDPR), public authorities performing their tasks can't rely on legitimate interests, so booking data rests on the public-task basis [S42]. The ICO lists automated decisions that affect access to a service, and online services offered directly to children, among processing likely to need a DPIA, and recommends one "if in any doubt" [S43]. A suspension applied automatically after three no-shows is an automated decision. Where such a decision is significant, the law requires safeguards: information about the decision, a chance to make representations, human intervention and a way to contest it. The ICO's guidance on this is still in draft [S44].

**F-21 The library duty and equality law require routes for people who can't book online.** The council must provide "a comprehensive and efficient library service for all persons desiring to make use thereof" [S45]. Government guidance reads "comprehensive" as a service "accessible to all residents using reasonable means, including digital technologies", and says access problems linked to disability or age must be "capable of being met" [S46]. The Equality Act 2010 requires reasonable adjustments for disabled people, including information in an accessible format [S47], and due regard to equality of opportunity in all the council's functions [S48].

**F-22 The ICO children's code doesn't apply, but UK GDPR does.** The ICO says a public authority's online public service that is not typically provided commercially is not an "information society service", so the Age Appropriate Design Code doesn't apply to it [S49]. Members of any age may book (Q-3), so children's booking data is still personal data under UK GDPR and belongs in the DPIA (F-20).

### 5.7 RQ-7: Availability and speed targets

**F-23 A 99.5% monthly availability floor is modest for a hosted service.** One hosted-software supplier commits to 99.9% availability measured each calendar month, excluding maintenance announced at least three days ahead [S50]. Large services can still fall short: Microsoft 365 recorded 99.526% in the first quarter of 2026, the lowest figure the reporting site has recorded since 2013 [S51]. AS-14 (at least 99.5% a month, with maintenance outside opening hours and 5 working days' notice) is therefore achievable. The library-specific suppliers I reviewed don't publish service levels (§9), so suppliers should state theirs in their responses.

**F-24 Google's Core Web Vitals give a published speed benchmark.** A page is "good" when, at the 75th percentile of page views, the largest content element appears within 2.5 seconds (Largest Contentful Paint, LCP), the page responds to input within 200 milliseconds (Interaction to Next Paint, INP) and layout shift is 0.1 or less (Cumulative Layout Shift, CLS) [S52]. AS-15 (95% of pages within 2 seconds) is stricter than this benchmark. Either is measurable; the specification must state which applies and how it is measured.

**F-25 The load is small (estimate).** Basis: about 300 bookings a month [S1], or about 10 a day over a 30-day month, spread across six rooms. AS-15's 50 people at once is far above this, so capacity is not a selection risk. The rate in the peak months is not known.

### 5.8 RQ-8: How the council can buy

**F-26 A small contract is likely below the main thresholds, with notice duties from £30,000.** The Procurement Act 2023 came into force on 24 February 2025 [S53]; sections 85–88 govern regulated below-threshold contracts [S54]. For contracting authorities other than central government, a contract worth £30,000 or more including value added tax (VAT) is "notifiable". The council must then publish a below-threshold tender notice on the central digital platform before advertising anywhere else, unless it invites quotes only from pre-selected suppliers. It can't use a separate pre-qualification stage, must allow a reasonable time to respond, and must publish a contract details notice, normally within 90 days [S55]. This confirms the Q-7 basis.

**F-27 A framework route exists, but no booking service found on it offers library-card sign-in.** G-Cloud 15, run by the Government Commercial Agency (which replaced the Crown Commercial Service on 1 April 2026), covers hosted software and support, is open to local authorities, and starts on 6 August 2026 [S56]. The page gives an end date of 5 February 2028 but also says it runs "for 4 years from 6 August 2026", so procurement should check the current term. A search of the G-Cloud 15 catalogue on 10 October 2026 found no listing for the two library booking products reviewed, LibCal and Communico Reserve [S83][S84]. A search for "SIP2" returned only two library management systems, Civica Spydus and a Bondap system [S82]. The booking services returned for "library booking" include LemonBooking and SIM Resource Booking [S81], which lack library-card sign-in (F-14). No booking service found on G-Cloud 15 therefore meets the Q-8 sign-in default without extra integration, so a quotation exercise is the more likely route (C-13). Catalogue searches match listing text only, so procurement should repeat the check before choosing the route (specification OQ-4).

**F-28 Requiring Cyber Essentials follows central-government policy.** Procurement Policy Note (PPN) 014 (February 2025) tells central government to require Cyber Essentials for contracts where a supplier handles citizens' personal information, proportionately and renewed every year; other public bodies "may wish to apply the approach" [S57]. The Q-10 default applies it to this contract.

**F-29 Timeline evidence is thin.** No source gives typical set-up times for these products. Going live by 1 March 2027 leaves under five months for buying, connecting the library system, testing and training (§8).

### 5.9 RQ-9: Self check-in with a printed QR code

**F-30 QR check-in tied to a signed-in member is common practice, but a static code can be shared.** Newcastle and Sussex put a QR code at each space [S7][S8]. Cambridge's booking system requires the member to sign in and hold a confirmed booking before a scan counts [S58]. A 2023 peer-reviewed study of attendance systems treats QR-code sharing as a known form of cheating and counters it with location checks, codes that change, and registering the device [S59]. Stronger options cost more: tablets outside rooms that take check-in in place [S22][S21] and door locks with booking-specific codes [S21]. I found no data on how often printed codes are misused in libraries (§9).

### 5.10 RQ-10: Security baseline and sensitive details

**F-31 Secure web connections and encrypted storage are the government baseline.** GOV.UK's Service Manual says "you must make sure your service (including APIs) is only accessible through secure connections. For web-based services this means HTTPS only" [S71]. HTTPS (Hypertext Transfer Protocol Secure) is the secure form of the web protocol, which encrypts traffic using TLS. The NCSC says all versions of SSL (Secure Sockets Layer, the predecessor of TLS) "must not be used", that TLS 1.0 and 1.1 "should not be used", that TLS 1.2 "is still considered to provide strong protection where appropriate cryptographic choices are made", and that government systems should move to TLS 1.3 [S70]. The NCSC's cloud security principles say data "should be adequately protected against tampering and eavesdropping as it transits networks", and list encryption, secure erasure and service resilience among the protections for stored data [S72]. Together with F-17, this means every connection the booking service makes, with members, with staff and with the library management system, should be encrypted, and stored data should be encrypted too.

**F-32 Multi-factor sign-in by text message is the weakest option.** The NCSC ranks multi-factor methods by strength, from FIDO2 security keys (physical keys that use the FIDO Alliance's FIDO2 standard for phishing-resistant sign-in) and authenticator apps down to message-based methods (email, text and phone call), which "are only likely to be appropriate when no other strengthening method is possible" [S75]. The US standard for digital identity likewise says "use of the PSTN [public switched telephone network, which carries texts and calls] for out-of-band verification is restricted", and requires that other authenticator types be available to all users [S76]. The Q-10 default asks only for multi-factor sign-in for staff; this evidence supports requiring an authenticator app or stronger method, with text-message codes at most a fallback.

**F-33 Reasons staff record for overriding a rule can become health data.** Under UK GDPR Article 9(1), processing "data concerning health" is prohibited unless a listed condition applies [S74]. Where a council relies on some of those conditions, such as substantial public interest, it must also meet a condition in Schedule 1 to the Data Protection Act 2018, which section 10 of the Act links to Article 9 [S77]. The ICO says health data includes "any information on injury, disease, disability or disease risk", and that data revealing or concerning health, including inferences, can count [S73]. A note that a member needs a longer booking because of a named condition would therefore be special category data. Recording only that a reasonable adjustment was made (AS-06), with no health detail, keeps to the data-minimisation principle [S65] and keeps such data out of the booking system.

## 9. Limitations

- **No study of library no-shows.** I found no peer-reviewed study of no-show rates, check-in or suspensions in library room booking. Healthcare reminder evidence stands in (F-7, F-9), so the size of the effect is uncertain.
- **Few English public-library examples.** Only one English public library's online booking page could be verified (F-4). Some council pages refused access and were excluded (§4).
- **Supplier capabilities are claims.** Product functions come from supplier documents and framework listings, not tests. The two library-specific suppliers reviewed don't publish prices; the one UK product with published prices lacks library sign-in (F-12, F-14).
- **The G-Cloud check is a keyword search.** It used four searches (F-27); a product described in other words could be missed.
- **The library management system is not named.** I could not check whether its supplier sells a room-booking module or supports encrypted SIP2 or an API, whether the system locks a PIN after failed attempts, how its PINs are set (chosen by members, a default such as the date of birth, or with common PINs refused) or how long they are (F-13, F-18, F-34). How PINs are set matters more than their length to the protection a cap of 20 guesses gives (F-34).
- **Card-and-PIN sign-in falls short of NIST as a single factor.** NIST requires a password used as the only factor, including a numeric PIN, to be at least 15 characters (§3.1.1, §3.1.1.2) [S76]; the sign-in limit reduces the risk but doesn't remove it (F-34). Card and PIN is kept because it is the owner-approved default (Q-8), PINs are set and held by the library management system, and English libraries use it [S32][S33][S34]; council IT accepts the remaining risk when it validates the cap (specification AS-34).
- **PIN-guessing figures come from other settings.** The 5–8% estimate rests on PINs chosen for unlocking phones [S88], and the birthday finding on banking PINs [S89]. No study of library PINs was found.
- **No published service levels from library-specific suppliers.** Availability benchmarks come from other hosted services (F-23).
- **No evidence on set-up times** for these products (F-29), and none on how much front-desk time online booking saves (F-15).
- **No benchmark for the 60% self-service share** (Q-1): I found no published share of online bookings for library rooms.
- **Draft regulatory guidance.** The ICO's guidance on automated decisions is in draft (F-20). Whether a four-week online-booking suspension is a "significant" decision is for the council's data protection officer to judge.
- **Pandemic-era data.** The Bodleian no-show figure comes from 2021 seat booking, reported by two student newspapers from one statement (F-11).
- **Ofcom's 2026 media-use report could not be opened**, so the government's report of Lloyds' and Ofcom's figures, and the Lloyds/Ipsos survey, were used instead (F-6).
